Where the groups come from

The checks are grouped the way the data quality literature groups them, rather than the way the code happens to be written. Wang and Strong (1996) interviewed data users and found they judge quality on more than whether the numbers are right: they also ask whether the data suits the job, whether it is clearly presented, and whether it can be reached and believed. Pipino, Lee and Wang (2002) turned those into measurable ratios, which is what a check like "how many rows carry a source" actually is. Batini and colleagues (2009) surveyed thirteen assessment methods and found they share the same three steps: describe the data, measure it against stated rules, then decide what to do about the failures. That is the shape of this page.

Three rules I worked to

rulewhy
A wrong value is worse than a blank.A blank tells a reader to go and look. A wrong value tells them nothing is missing. Every fix in VA3 that removes a value rather than mending it comes from this.
Check the output, not the intention.Every script in this project reported success while writing files that were wrong. The only thing worth trusting is the file itself, read back afterwards.
Write down what failed, not only what passed.The value of this page is the nine things that were wrong, not the thirty-nine that were fine.
Clean each source before joining them, not after. Rahm and Do (2000) make this point and it decided the shape of the whole pipeline: the three files were each cleaned on their own terms, and only then merged. Joining first would have mixed three sets of problems together and made every one of them harder to see.

CD3 · When a value is good enough to keep

Most of the work in this project is not fetching values. It is deciding which of the values that come back deserve to be written down. Every rule below started as something that went wrong.

An ingredient list is recognised by its shape, not its heading

A page that says "Ingredients" underneath a paragraph of marketing does not have an ingredient list on it. So the text is judged on what it looks like:

testwhy
at least five pieces separated by commasa formula is a list, and a sentence is not
at least three of them recognisable chemical namesthe benefits column was passing as ingredients until this was added, because "Hydrates, Soothes, Brightens" is also a comma separated list
a gap of up to two odd pieces is allowedan early version took the longest unbroken run and one strange name in the middle cut real formulas in half
refused if three or more pieces are benefit wordsthe same reason as above, caught from the other direction
The validator went through six rounds. The version that damaged data was the one that looked most careful: it kept only the longest run of clean pieces, which sounds strict and quietly deleted the second half of hundreds of formulas. Being strict is not the same as being right.

A claim about absence needs a complete list

Saying a product is fragrance free is a claim about what is not in it, and that can only be checked against a whole formula. So free_from is only written where the list has at least eight ingredients and does not trail off with "and 25 more". Everywhere else it stays empty, which is why 864 products have an ingredient list but no free-from value.

"Not for oily skin" does not mean oily skin

Reading skin type out of a sentence catches negations if nobody checks. You asked whether it did, and it had: thirteen products carried the opposite of what their page said. "Not Good for Oily Skin" had been read as Oily.

Two of my first three catches were wrong, which is why the rule now looks at what the negation applies to rather than just spotting the word "not".

1 · Why I threw the skin type away and started again

This is the honest version. The first column was not wrong in a small, fixable way. It was built on a foundation my supervisors were right to question, so I deleted it and rebuilt it from nothing.

What the old column was actually made of

It looked like one variable. It was four different kinds of claim sharing a single heading.

The measurement that condemned it. I measured how often these sources agreed with each other, four separate times, using different subsets and different matching thresholds. Every measurement landed near 50%.

That number is the entire argument. If two sources claiming to measure the same thing agree half the time, then either one of them is wrong half the time, or they are not measuring the same thing. Four kinds of evidence poured into one column. That is what the 50% agreement was really telling me.

Why this matters: it is the empirical justification for building a dataset instead of reusing one, and it belongs in the methodology chapter as a stated finding rather than an assumption.

Attempt 2 · Derive the skin type from the ingredients
EU CosIng function field · 79 functions · 28,492 of 28,710 entries
Map every ingredient to its official CosIng function, then reason from function to skin type. Humectants, emollients and occlusives point toward dry skin; astringents and sebum regulators toward oily. It is defensible chemistry and I already had the mapping built from the INCI standardisation work.

I rejected it on principle, not on difficulty. It produces an inference, which is exactly what my supervisors objected to in SkinCarisma. Doing the inference myself would not turn it into a fact. It would only make it my inference instead of somebody else's.

It still has real value as a validation layer. If the manufacturer says "for dry skin" and the formula is humectant-heavy, that agreement means something. That is a legitimate future chapter.

Attempt 3 · Visit every brand website directly
three full passes · reached 6.6% of products
Guess the brand's domain from its name, walk the site, find the product page. This gets exactly the right kind of evidence, straight from the manufacturer. It simply cannot find the pages.

Brand sites are hostile to this approach: URLs are unguessable, catalogues render in JavaScript so the downloaded HTML contains nothing, sites split by region (us., uk., .co.uk), and product naming differs between the brand's own site and every other source. After three passes it had covered 6.6%. The evidence type was right and the coverage was hopeless.

Attempt 4 · Scrape a search engine
DuckDuckGo → ConnectTimeout on every request
If I cannot guess the URL, let a search engine find it for me. DuckDuckGo returned a connection timeout every single time. Not slow, not rate limited, refused at the network level before any page loaded.
Attempt 5 · Try all the other engines
Bing, Mojeek, Startpage → captcha
The same wall in a different shape. This is the point at which I understood the real problem, and the diagnosis is the important part of this whole page:

My network blocks automated access to search engines. A person typing in a browser gets results. A script does not. Search engines block automated traffic deliberately, because it consumes serving capacity and generates no advertising revenue.

Understanding why I was blocked is what pointed at the solution. You cannot route around this by writing better scraping code, because the block is not in my code. You have to stop scraping.

Attempt 6 · A search API — Serper
paid service · official Google results · the method in the dataset
I watched a tutorial on how automated search is normally done and learned that nobody scrapes search engines. They call a search API: a paid, permitted, documented door to the same results.

Serper returns real Google results as clean structured data. There is no captcha because I am not pretending to be a browser. I am a customer with a key, using a commercial service the way it is designed to be used. Page 3 explains exactly what it does and, just as importantly, what it does not do.

4 · The four tiers of evidence

Not every website deserves the same trust. Every row records which kind of source answered it, so any figure in the thesis can be recomputed at whatever strictness a reader wants.

Every filled row sits on exactly one of these rungs.

Why tier 1 is the strongest evidence that exists

Under EU Regulation 1223/2009 the manufacturer is legally responsible for the claims it makes about a cosmetic product. A statement on the brand's own website is therefore not marketing opinion in the way a magazine review is. It is a claim the company can be held to. That is the reason this tier sits at the top, and it is a better answer than "because the brand knows its own product".

Why a retailer claim is not the same as an analysis site

A retailer reports. Shop listings are, overwhelmingly, the brand's own product copy passed along. The retailer is repeating a declared claim, not forming one. That is why it is tier 2 and not tier 3.
An analysis site concludes. SkinCarisma and INCIDecoder read the ingredient list and reason to a skin type. It may well be good reasoning, but it is a third party's conclusion, not anybody's claim about the product.

How a shop is recognised, and why the method changed

My first version used a hand-written list of about sixty shop names. That was a bad design for two reasons: it silently discarded any shop I had not personally heard of, and that bias runs precisely against a thesis covering Lebanese retail.

Why blank sensitivity became "Resistant"

Sensitivity was blank on most rows after searching. Blank does not mean unknown here. Brands advertise a sensitive-skin claim when they have one, because it sells; they do not hide it. So after a thorough search the absence of that claim is itself informative, and Resistant is the other half of the Baumann axis.

This is an assumption, it is stated openly, and it is reversible. The tier and quote columns show exactly which rows carry a positive sensitivity finding (2,221 products) as opposed to an inferred Resistant (5,199).
A caveat I should raise before anyone else does. Vendruscolo et al. (2025, Dermatological Reviews 6:e70045) show that "suitable for all skin types" is a tolerance-testing claim derived from repeat insult patch testing, not a dermatological classification. The same paper found that 89% of 187 products labelled hypoallergenic still contained at least one recognised contact allergen. So All must be read as "the manufacturer makes a universal claim", which is exactly what the column records, and not as clinical evidence of universal suitability.

I graded a domain as "the manufacturer" only if the brand name, punctuation stripped, appeared inside it. That fails constantly. 2 · I never asked the brand's own website
the largest error, and the reason 1,160 rows looked unfixable

Every early pass searched by keyword and hoped the manufacturer would rank highly. It often does not, because ingredient-analysis sites are better optimised for a query containing the words "skin type" than brand sites are. So the searches kept returning exactly the sources I was trying to avoid.

The fix was to learn each brand's domain once and then ask that domain directly with site:. Of 893 products I had described as unfixable, 652 had an official or retail page all along, 316 on the brand's own site and 336 in a shop. I was wrong about the data. The data was not wrong about me.

3 · A verification check silently discarded good pages
found by re-reading my own filter, not from any error message

After fetching a page I required the squashed brand name to appear in the HTML, to be sure I had the right product. But "Dear, Klairs" squashes to dearklairs, which appears nowhere, because the page says "Klairs". Real manufacturer pages were being thrown away without a single warning.

Now any distinctive word of the brand counts, and a page on the brand's own domain is trusted without the check. This is a good example to offer in a viva of a bug that produces no error and quietly costs coverage: the kind you only ever find by reading your own code.

4 · Some quotes were raw page markup
found by reading a random sample of rows instead of trusting the count

Roughly a quarter of the stored sentences were fragments of HTML rather than readable English, such as "></div> </h1> <div class="marginb23">. Every quote is now cleaned, and anything still unreadable is emptied rather than shown, on the principle that a blank cell is more honest than a broken one. The link is always kept, so the evidence survives even where the sentence did not, and a free follow-up pass re-opened the known URLs to recover clean sentences.

Today 6,928 of 7,420 filled rows carry a readable sentence, and 7,410 carry a working link.

5 · "Out of credits" was being recorded as "no answer"
1,052 rows affected

When an API key ran out, Serper returned HTTP 400. My code did not recognise 400 as a key problem, so it filed the result as "this product has no answer" and then skipped that product on the next run. Those products would have been permanently and invisibly missing.

Technical failures are now never written as findings. A network problem can never masquerade as a fact about a product. This is the single most dangerous class of bug in a scraping pipeline, because it is silent and it looks like data.

6 · Smaller corrections, listed for completeness
each one found by testing a piece of the pipeline in isolation

• \boil\b does not match "oily", so switched to prefix matching. This had been silently undercounting every sentence containing the word.
• The code stored the matched word rather than the sentence around it, so quotes were meaningless out of context.
• Six concurrent workers times three queries was dropping connections and producing 1,031 spurious "network error" rows. Reduced to three workers with three retries and exponential backoff.
• mark_not_applicable only wrote a status into empty cells, so after its first run it never updated again and kept reporting stale coverage long after 1,182 more products had been filled.
• A merge dropped 893 sensitivity-only rows because it required a skin_type value to be present, overstating the gap by 1,066.

Stage 1, Blocking

Papadakis et al. 2020, ACM Computing Surveys 53(2), "Blocking and Filtering Techniques for Entity Resolution: A Survey". naive matching is quadratic, every record against every record, and becomes infeasible. the solution is a cheap blocking key so only records sharing it are compared. the trade-off is measured by pair completeness (did the blocks keep the true matches?) versus reduction ratio (how much work saved?).

15,788 Skinsort products x ~10,800,000 Amazon products

The distance metrics, in full

Everything below is built on one base formula. The library is rapidfuzz; the academic justification is Cohen 2003 for the choice and Levenshtein / Jaro-Winkler for the origins.

The base

ratio(a, b) = 2 × M / ( len(a) + len(b) ) × 100     M = matching characters

token_sort_ratio(a,b) = ratio( sort_words(a), sort_words(b) )      fixes word ORDER
token_set_ratio(a,b)  = max ratio over {shared, shared+extra_a, shared+extra_b}   ignores EXTRA words

token_sort_ratio worked out in full

a = "CeraVe Moisturizing Cream"      b = "Moisturizing Cream CeraVe"

1. tokens(a) = [cerave, moisturizing, cream]
   tokens(b) = [cerave, cream, moisturizing]
2. sort  a' = "cerave cream moisturizing"
         b' = "cerave cream moisturizing"     now identical
3. ratio(a', b') = 100        plain ratio on the same pair is only 72

Fixes word ORDER. Extra words still hurt → use for DE-DUPLICATION.

token_set_ratio worked out in full

a = "lip care"     b = "beesline lip care shimmery strawberry"

set(a) = {care, lip}      set(b) = {beesline, care, lip, shimmery, strawberry}
shared    = "care lip"
only_in_a = ""                                  a adds nothing new
only_in_b = "beesline shimmery strawberry"

t0 = shared                = "care lip"
t1 = shared + only_in_a    = "care lip"
t2 = shared + only_in_b    = "care lip beesline shimmery strawberry"

token_set_ratio = max( ratio(t0,t1), ratio(t0,t2), ratio(t1,t2) ) = 100

because t0 and t1 are both just the shared words, they are identical, so the max is 100. it IGNORES the extra words by design. perfect for matching across sites; dangerous for dedup.

Once the brand and the pack size are stripped out, product names are short and share a lot of boilerplate, so the score is high for almost any two products of the same brand. Merging transitively, where A matches B and B matches C so A joins C, then drags unrelated products into one group.

The rule that works. Two listings are the same product only if they share a brand and neither name carries a distinguishing word the other lacks. A variant always announces itself with a word: Tinted, Night, Mineral, SPF50, Light. So an unmatched word means a different product.
Two details that each cost me wrong merges.

• Short words had to be kept. An early version dropped words under three characters as noise, which merged "Ruboril Expert M" with "Ruboril Expert S", and "Xerolys 50" with "Xerolys 10". The variant marker is very often the shortest word on the label.

• Accents were breaking brand matching. "Avène" squashed to avne and "L'Oréal" to loral, so accented brands could never merge across shops. Brand names are now folded to plain ASCII, and prefixes are stripped, because "Avène" and "Eau Thermale Avène" are one brand, as are "Aderma" and "A-Derma", and "Aloelab" and "The Aloelab".

Why the same product appears six times

A shop is not a manufacturer. Six shops selling CeraVe all list the same cream, each with their own wording, their own size in the title and their own spelling. Counting them as six products would say something false about the Lebanese market, so they have to become one row.

Four listings, one product, no information lost

How two listings are judged to be the same product

Matching by exact text finds almost nothing, because no two shops write a name the same way. The method comes from the record linkage literature and runs in three steps.

stepwhat happenswhere it comes from
1. make the names comparableaccents removed, punctuation removed, size and offer wording stripped, words sorted. "CeraVe Moisturising Lotion 236ml" and "Cerave Moisturizing Lotion 236 ML" both become cerave lotion moisturizingRahm and Do (2000), section 3.1, "data transformation", where they list standardisation of value formats as the step before any matching can work
2. only compare products of the same brandthe file is grouped by brand first, so a CeraVe lotion is never compared against a Nivea one. 1,596 brands means 1,596 small comparisons instead of one enormous oneChristen (2012), chapter 4, "indexing", called blocking. It exists to avoid comparing every record with every other record
3. two thresholds, not oneidentical word sets are accepted with no further checking. Anything scoring 90 or above on the remaining comparison is accepted, below that is left aloneFellegi and Sunter (1969), section 2, where they define an upper and a lower threshold with a review band between them

5a · What an ontology is, and why this dataset needs one

The dataset is a table. It can tell you that a product is for dry skin. It cannot tell you why, and it cannot work anything out on its own. An ontology writes the meaning down in a form a machine can follow, and that is the whole difference.

The definition I am working from

From the CMPS456 lecture, slide 4: an ontology turns the complexities of reality into a structured guide that helps computers understand and categorise knowledge about real-world objects. 5b · Ontologies that already exist

Slide 15 of the lecture is one word: reuse. It is worth being clear about what that means in practice, because it is not inspiration. When I reuse schema.org, the schema.org property goes inside my ontology and my file says schema:name where it would otherwise have said something I made up.

What reuse actually looks like

Instead of inventing a property of my own:

myOntology:hasProductName        <- invented, nobody else understands it

I use the one that already exists and is already understood:

schema:name                      <- standard, every tool knows it

So a product in my knowledge graph is written like this, using a real row from the file:

:LBO-00802
    a                  :Product ;
    schema:name        "The Aloelab 0.5% Retinol Night Serum" ;
    schema:brand       :TheAloelab ;
    schema:category    "Serum" ;
    :suitableFor       :DrySkin ;
    :contains          :Glycerin , :Niacinamide .

Three of those six lines are somebody else’s vocabulary. Only the ones starting with a bare colon are mine, and those are the ones nothing existing covers.

5e · Checking the ontology

The dataset passes 48 checks. The ontology will need its own, and they are a different kind: a dataset is checked for wrong values, an ontology is checked for a wrong model. Four methods, and they catch four different things.

1. Competency questions

Slide 14 of the lecture: write the questions the ontology must be able to answer before building it, then test it by asking them. Grüninger and Fox (1995) introduced this, and the point is that a question you cannot answer tells you exactly which class or property is missing.

question it must answerwhat it forces into the modelcan the dataset support it today?
Which products suit dry, sensitive skin?SkinType, Sensitivity, suitableForyes, 13184 products carry both
Which products contain no fragrance allergens?Ingredient, IngredientFunction, the CosIng linkonly after CosIng is loaded. This is the question that justifies reuse
Why does this product suit dry skin?Evidence, statedBy, quotedSentenceyes, 10620 products store the actual sentence
What does this cost in Lebanon, and elsewhere?Offer, seller, seenOnyes, 6 products carry both prices today, and more after the next run
Which Lebanese products have no reviews anywhere?Review, Brand, countryyes, and the answer is all 1,195 of them
Find a cheaper product like this one.contains, hasCategory, hasOffer togetheryes, this is the recommender question

2. The reasoner

HermiT or Pellet, run inside Protégé. It checks that the model does not contradict itself, and it finds contradictions a person reading the file would not.

what it catchesan example from this domain
a class that can have no membersif Sunscreen must have an SPF and SPF must be under 110, and I also wrote a rule saying sunscreens have SPF over 200, the class is empty and the reasoner says so
a cycle in the hierarchyslide 26. If Moisturiser is a kind of Cream and Cream is a kind of Moisturiser, nothing works
an individual in two classes that exclude each othera product classified as both Sunscreen and Cleanser when the model says a product has exactly one category
links that should follow but do notif Glycerin is a Humectant and Humectants suit dry skin, the reasoner can add "suits dry skin" to every product containing glycerin, and I can then check whether the dataset agrees

3. OOPS!, the pitfall scanner

A web tool that reads an ontology and reports common modelling mistakes. Poveda-Villalón, Gómez-Pérez and Suárez-Figueroa (2014) built a catalogue of 41 pitfalls from an empirical study of 693 ontologies, rated critical, important or minor, and the tool checks 33 of them automatically.

pitfall it looks forwhy it would apply to mine
P04, creating unconnected elementsa class nobody links to. Easy to leave behind when the model grows
P08, missing annotationsa class called Evidence with no description is useless to anybody but me
P11, missing domain or rangea property that could point at anything is not a constraint
P19, defining multiple domainssaying contains applies to both Product and Brand quietly breaks reasoning
P41, no licence declareda small thing, but it is the difference between reusable and not

The tool is at oops.linkeddata.es and the paper is in the International Journal on Semantic Web and Information Systems 10(2), 7–34.

4. SHACL, for the data rather than the model

The reasoner and OOPS! check the model. SHACL checks that the individuals obey it: every Product must have exactly one category, every Offer must have a price and a date, every suitableFor must have Evidence behind it. These are the same rules as my 48 dataset checks, written in a standard language instead of in Python. w3.org/TR/shacl

This is the neat part. My validation script and a SHACL shape would be saying the same thing in two languages. If they ever disagree, one of them has a bug, and that is a useful thing to be able to test.

5f · Using the ontology to check the dataset

This is the part I find most interesting, and it runs the usual direction backwards. The dataset is built first and the ontology second, so the ontology becomes a way of testing the dataset that could not have existed while the dataset was being made.

Why a reasoner finds things a checking script cannot

My 48 checks each look at one thing at a time: is this value in the list, is that number in range, does this column agree with that one. A reasoner looks at everything at once and follows chains, so it finds faults that only appear when three facts are put together.

Why price per millilitre is the one that matters

A 30 ml serum at $22 and a 100 ml serum at $30 look like the cheap one and the expensive one, until the size is taken into account, at which point they are the expensive one and the cheap one. A recommender without this figure will systematically push customers toward small packages. It is the field most often missing from retail data and the one a shop notices first.

What a bundle would have done to it

Size is refused whenever a name shows more than one, because “Serum 30 ML + Cream 50 ML” is a pair and neither number belongs to the price. An earlier version accepted “Eau Thermale 300ml + 300ml duo” as 300 ml because the two numbers agreed. The pack holds 600, so the product looked twice as expensive per millilitre as it is. That is precisely the error this column exists to prevent, so any second size, and any of duo, trio, pack of, x2, now refuses the reading outright.

A price nobody can date is a price nobody can judge, and Lebanese prices move. price_seen_date is what separates a snapshot from a feed.

Sensitivity is better evidenced than skin type

Irritants are named in law rather than inferred from marketing. A product is marked Resistant when its formula contains any of the 26 fragrance allergens the EU requires to be declared under Regulation 1223/2009 Annex III, or alcohol denat, or methylisothiazolinone, or an essential oil. It is marked Sensitive when a full formula contains none of them.

Where there is no formula to check, sensitivity defaults to Resistant. That is the cautious direction on purpose. Telling someone with sensitive skin that an unknown product is fine is the harmful error. Telling them to check is not.

Where two shops disagreed

201 products had two Lebanese shops state the same skin type. 174 had them state different ones. That is an agreement rate of 53.6%, and it is a measured statement about the reliability of Lebanese retail product data that very few datasets report.

Those conflicts are not settled by a vote. A vote treats every source as equally trustworthy and they are not: under Regulation 1223/2009 the manufacturer is responsible for what a product claims, and a shop is repeating that at second hand. So the brand is asked as a third source and its answer settles it outright. Only when the brand is silent does the value more shops stated win, and when the shops tie and the brand says nothing, nothing is changed and the conflict is recorded as unresolved. A coin toss written into a dataset is worse than an acknowledged conflict.

Who publishes, and who does not

Three separate attempts to fill the same gaps produced the same shape of answer, which is what makes it a finding rather than a shortfall.

An earlier version of this portal said Lebanese-made products have no reviews anywhere. The same pattern in ingredients

Under Regulation 1223/2009 Article 19 the ingredient declaration is the manufacturer’s responsibility. Formula coverage now stands at 11,050 (83.8%), and the products still without one are overwhelmingly artisanal makers and shop-own labels with no website to ask.

And in who will answer a machine at all

316 brand sites were asked for their sitemap. The brands that returned nothing were almost all the large multinationals: Dior, Clinique, Estée Lauder, La Mer, Kiehl’s, Lancôme, LUSH, Benefit, Bobbi Brown, Dove, La Roche-Posay. The independents handed over everything: Beesline 523 products, Khan El Kaser 1,100, Dermedic 638, Babaria 1,861.

This is the opposite of what is usually assumed. The brands with the most resources to publish structured product data are the ones that block access to it, and the smaller brands publish freely because they want to be found. It is also why a Lebanese recommender can cover local brands better than global luxury ones.

Products nobody is selling

The products that resisted every pass turned out to have something in common. Of those left without an image, only 3.5% had a price from anywhere, against 92.4% across the dataset. A product with no image, no price and no reviews is not a hole in the collection. It is a product that is not currently on sale.

Linked to the European Commission register

Every ingredient is now matched against CosIng, the Commission’s own inventory of cosmetic ingredients kept under Regulation (EC) 1223/2009. 10,876 of the 11,050 products with a formula matched, 98.4% of them.

Why this is the most valuable thing added

Until now the concerns column made an assertion. It said a product may worsen dryness, and behind that was a rule written for this project. It was a reasonable rule, but it was ours.

Now the same claim can name its evidence: the product contains Alcohol Denat, whose function the European Commission records as denaturant and solvent, with a CAS number and a register entry. The rule has a regulator behind it rather than an author. That is the difference between an assertion and a justified assertion, and it is the kind of thing an examiner asks about.

That is not a warning: phenoxyethanol and citric acid are restricted by concentration, not banned, and they are ordinary preservatives and pH adjusters. It is a column that lets somebody ask the question rather than an answer to it.

The published CSV carries seven lines of preamble before its real header, so a plain read produces a table whose only column is “File creation date”. That is how the Commission ships it. The header is found by looking for the line that names INCI.

Matching is exact, deliberately

Names are compared after lowercasing and removing brackets, because shops write Aqua (Water) where the register says AQUA. Nothing fuzzy is allowed. A chemical either is the registered name or it is not, and a near miss on a chemical name is a different chemical.

What this sets up

CosIng is the source CosIng-KG is built from, so these ingredients now carry the identifiers that ontology already uses. The link between this dataset and the ontology phase is no longer something to design later. It exists in the file.
